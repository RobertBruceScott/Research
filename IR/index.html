A / PREMISE

The difficult question is not whether radiation can damage matter. It is how a measurable physical event propagates through repair, tissue biology and decades of life.

Low environmental doses are far below those associated with immediate tissue reactions. For long-term effects such as cancer, however, a universally risk-free threshold has not been demonstrated. Radiation protection therefore works with population evidence, explicit assumptions and uncertainty.

B / EXPOSURE FIELDS

The background is not one thing.

Source, energy spectrum, route of exposure and the affected tissue all matter.

μ

EXTERNAL FIELD

Cosmic radiation

Primary particles meet the atmosphere and generate a mixed field of secondary particles. Exposure changes with altitude, latitude and solar conditions.

Rn

INTERNAL EXPOSURE

Radon

A radioactive gas produced in the uranium decay chain. Its short-lived progeny can be inhaled and irradiate the respiratory tract.

γ

LOCAL GEOLOGY

Terrestrial radiation

Uranium, thorium and potassium-40 in rocks, soils and building materials contribute to the local background field.

D / HEALTH EFFECTS

One track.
Many possible fates.

Ionization is a physical event. Health is an emergent outcome shaped by spatial damage, repair, tissue context, time and individual susceptibility.

01THRESHOLD-LIKE

Tissue reactions

At sufficiently high dose, enough cells are injured to impair tissue function. Severity rises with dose above an endpoint-specific threshold.

02STOCHASTIC RISK

Cancer and heritable effects

Protection models treat cancer as a probability that may increase with dose. At low dose, the increment is difficult to distinguish epidemiologically from baseline variation.

03ACTIVE RESEARCH

Ageing and non-cancer outcomes

DNA damage, inflammation and epigenetic change are biologically connected, but environmental-dose effects and their clinical importance require careful causal evidence.

INDIVIDUAL VARIATION

The “average person” is a model, not a patient.

Radiation-protection quantities are designed for populations. Rare inherited defects in DNA-damage signalling or repair can produce much larger biological effects than common variants, but sensitivity is pathway-, tissue- and endpoint-specific.

HIGH-PENETRANCE CONDITIONS

DNA-damage response syndromes

Examples include ataxia-telangiectasia, Nijmegen breakage syndrome, LIG4 syndrome and some Fanconi-anaemia disorders. Clinical decisions require specialist assessment.

A USEFUL CAVEAT

Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP)

XP is defined chiefly by defective repair of UV-induced DNA lesions. Marked sensitivity to ionizing radiation is not a universal feature and varies with complementation group and experimental system.

AGEING BIOLOGY

Damage and epigenetics

Persistent lesions and repair can reshape chromatin and transcription; altered chromatin can in turn change damage recognition and repair. Neither process alone explains normal ageing.
